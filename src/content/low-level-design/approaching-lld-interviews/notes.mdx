## Overview

Start by understanding the problem, then design and test a small working solution. Do not rush straight to code.

An LLD interview usually gives you 45 to 60 minutes and a short, vague prompt such as "design a vending machine" or "design a meeting room scheduler". The interviewer is not looking for one correct answer. They want to watch you **turn ambiguity into a clear, working object model** and explain your choices.

A repeatable process keeps you from freezing or diving into code too early. The Case study tab has the same steps as a checklist you can practise with.

## Key ideas

A practical sequence, with rough timings for a 45-minute round:

1. **Clarify requirements (5 min).** Ask about core features, users, scale, and what is out of scope. Write a short list of functional and non-functional requirements and confirm it.
2. **Identify entities and actors (5 min).** Pull out the nouns: people, things, events. Separate entities (have identity) from value objects (defined by their values).
3. **Assign responsibilities (5 min).** For each class, one sentence: what it knows and what it does. Spot what varies and plan an interface for it.
4. **Define relationships and a class diagram (5 to 10 min).** Who owns whom, who uses whom, and multiplicities. Keep it readable; key methods only.
5. **Walk through the main flow (5 min).** Narrate the calls for the main use case, like a sequence diagram. This often reveals missing classes or methods.
6. **Implement the core (10 to 15 min).** Code the central classes and the main flow cleanly, with type hints. Stub the rest.
7. **Handle edge cases and errors (5 min).** Invalid input, concurrency, empty or full states, cancellations.
8. **Discuss extensions and trade-offs (remaining time).** How you would add a new type or feature; what you chose not to do and why.

Throughout: **think aloud**, check in with the interviewer at each step, and keep a visible list of assumptions.

## Example

Prompt: "Design a meeting room scheduler."

- *Clarify*: rooms have capacity; bookings have start and end times; no overlapping bookings per room; recurring meetings out of scope; single office.
- *Entities*: `Room`, `Booking`, `User`; `TimeSlot` as a value object.
- *Responsibilities*: `Room` knows its bookings and answers `is_free(slot)`; `Scheduler` finds a room with enough capacity and books it; `TimeSlot.overlaps(other)` holds the overlap rule.
- *Varies*: room selection rule (smallest fitting room, nearest room), so a `RoomSelectionStrategy`.
- *Main flow*: `Scheduler.book(user, slot, attendees)` filters rooms by capacity, asks each `is_free`, applies the strategy, creates the `Booking`.
- *Edge cases*: back-to-back meetings (end equals start is allowed), two users booking the last room at once (lock per room), invalid slots (end before start).
- *Extensions*: recurring bookings as a generator of `TimeSlot`s; notifications via Observer.

## Common mistakes

- **Coding in the first five minutes**, before requirements are agreed.
- **Silent thinking**: the interviewer cannot give credit for reasoning they cannot hear.
- **Trying to model everything**: twenty classes with no working flow scores worse than six classes that run.
- **Ignoring hints**: when the interviewer asks "what if two people book at once?", that is the next thing to address.
- **Pattern name-dropping** without explaining what problem the pattern solves here.
- **Never testing the design**: not walking through a flow or edge case before declaring it done.

## Interview angle

Interviewers usually score along a few lines: requirement gathering, object modelling and responsibilities, use of principles and patterns where they help, code quality, handling of edge cases, and communication. The process above touches each one deliberately.

Phrases that help: "Let me confirm scope before I design", "I'll put this behind an interface because it's likely to change", "Let me walk through the booking flow to check the design", and "Given more time, I would...".
